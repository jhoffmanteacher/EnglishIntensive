A browser-based practice site for older students working on decoding and sight words. Six word families, seven ways to practice each one, a microphone that listens to the student read, and a teacher dashboard that says which students to reteach and what to reteach them.

The basic shape

One word list, several different questions to ask about it.

A student signs in and sees only the lists their teacher has assigned them, so two students in the same room can be working on different things. Each list can be practiced in more than one mode — and each mode is tracked as its own skill, because reading moist cold, spelling it, and picking it out of a row of look-alikes are three separate things to be good at. A student can be solid at one and shaky at another, and the site keeps them apart.

Rounds are 18 words, not the whole list, and the words are chosen for that student (see How a round gets picked below).

Why the nonsense list has no Match It

Match It works by saying a word and asking the student to find it. Handed "vab", a speech synthesizer guesses — often as "verb" — so the answer key would be wrong. A list with no meanings can be read, not heard.

The seven ways to practice

Every mode scores the same way — points, streaks, and 0–3 stars — so a student moving between them meets one game asking different questions.

When a student gets it wrong

A red ✗ says they were wrong. It doesn't say which part — and for a reader guessing at blends or sliding off vowels, that's the only part worth knowing.

On the reveal, Say it lines up what the microphone heard against the word on screen, sound by sound, and names one thing. Never a list of three — a wrong reading usually has one cause, and three guesses at once is how a hint becomes noise. The letters that spell the sound in question light up in gold.

Real phoneme audio, not "buh"

A speech synthesizer asked for an isolated /b/ says "buh" — and "buh-a-tuh" does not blend into bat. So the sounds come from recorded clips instead, one for each of the 37 sounds the site can name, with the stop consonants trimmed back to the burst. Anywhere a sound is played, it is a real sound.

On the back of a flash card and on the Say it reveal, each syllable — or each sound in a one-syllable word — is pressable. Press it and it plays itself, then the whole word. A student who has been told a word twice and still can't read it usually can't hear which part they're getting wrong; pressing "tas" and hearing /t/ /a/ /s/ answers that without anyone having to explain it.

Hear yourself

After two misses, a button plays the last few seconds back and then reads the word properly. Students hear the word correctly in their own head the whole time; there is no arguing with the recording. The clip is never stored or uploaded — it exists in memory while that card is on screen and is destroyed when the next one arrives.

Red words and heart letters

A word is irregular, but it is rarely irregular all the way through.

said is s + d with one impossible middle. could is c + d with one. The back of the card marks that middle in red, under "In red: the part to remember by heart" — while the front stays plain, because the card has to be read cold first.

The point is the size of the thing being memorized. "Learn s-a-i-d" is four letters with no pattern. "Learn that said has an ai in the middle" is one chunk, and the s and the d are just reading. The marking is done by hand, deliberately — which part can't be sounded out is a teaching judgement, not something software should guess.

How a round gets picked

Eighteen words drawn from the list, weighted by how that student is actually doing.

  • How badly it's going. A word missed most of the time is about six times as likely to come up as one that's solid. Accuracy is smoothed, so a single lucky guess isn't mastery and a single slip isn't a crisis.
  • Whether it's had its rest. Each word sits in a spaced-repetition box earning breaks of 0, 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 days. Inside its break a word is quieted rather than banned, so short sessions don't loop the same handful.
  • First tries only. Getting a word right after being told it isn't knowing it. And a miss drops a word one box rather than back to zero — resetting turns one fumble into a week of seeing that word every session.
  • Speed, on the cards. A word decoded in three seconds is not a sight word, however reliably it comes back right. Flips are timed; over 2.5 seconds a word counts as slow and keeps coming round after its accuracy has stopped moving.
Slow is the scheduler's business, and yours

Students see a "⚡ fast" pill for a quick flip, and there is deliberately no slow pill — being told you were slow makes the next card slower, not faster. Slow shows up on your screen instead: the per-list breakdown reads "12 / 20 (4 slow)", and nobody is suggested for the next list on the strength of thirty words they can decode but not read.

Two extras sit on the start screen: a 🔁 Comeback words warm-up of whatever this student has been missing lately, and — once eight words are solid — a ⚡ Speed round of nothing but words they already own, cards flipping themselves after two seconds. Useless as practice; the only thing on the site that asks whether a word can be read without thinking about it.

The one-minute word-rate check

Every other mode asks whether a student knows a word. This one asks how fast — the question that goes on being worth asking long after accuracy has stopped moving.

Words in rows of five, sixty seconds, the deck cycling so a fast reader never runs out. Scored in correct words per minute, with the change since last time, which is the reason to do it twice. Targets are 60 CWPM on real words and 40 on the nonsense list. The clock starts on the first word actually read, not on the button — a student fumbling with headphones for four seconds has not been reading for four seconds.

The listening is built to survive the way these students actually read. A hesitation, a repeated word, a false start, a skipped word, or the recognizer splitting "hubcap" into "hub cap" does not knock the rest of the run out of alignment; a genuine misread still counts as one. Every run is kept — up to a term's worth — and the dashboard draws each list as a small trend line, because a single latest number says nothing about whether anything is changing.

It's a rate, not fluency in the full sense

A list of words measures automaticity of word recognition — whether a word comes back without effort. That's a real construct, and the one timed word-list tests like TOWRE assess. It is not prosody, phrasing, or holding meaning across a sentence, because none of those exist outside connected text.

The two numbers also differ, and the gap is informative: a passage hands a weak decoder context to lean on, and a list takes it away. That's the point of doing it this way — it isolates decoding speed from compensating strategies — but it means a good rate here is not evidence that a paragraph goes well. Passage reading wants its own probe.

Built for these readers specifically

The audience is 9th and 10th graders practicing skills usually taught much younger.

  • Nothing is coded elementary. No grade labels, no cartoon imagery, no "Big Words" — the list is called Multisyllable. The progress graphics are a race car and a neon vault run.
  • 👁 Reading view, on every start screen. Four switches: font (site default, Lexend, or Atkinson Hyperlegible — drawn by the Braille Institute so b/d/p/q and I/l/1 can be told apart), wide letter spacing, all-lowercase, and cream cards instead of dark. The fonts ship with the site, so a school network that blocks Google Fonts can't silently take them away from the one student who needs them.
  • 🔊 Read directions aloud on every game, in the same voice as the words. The how-to-play copy is written short and plain on purpose: this is a phonics class, and the instructions should not themselves be a decoding challenge.
  • Right and wrong are shown, not just heard — a green ✓ or red ✗ over the word — so nothing depends on a student hearing anything. The spoken coach is off by default.
  • Silence is never a wrong answer, and neither is a cough or an "um" — the mic modes excuse a response that is entirely noise rather than marking a word nobody tried to read.
  • Mic-free options everywhere. Cards and Match It work in a loud room and on a Chromebook with a dead microphone.

What the teacher side does

One dashboard, four tabs, and a bias toward showing you the next action rather than every number.

Setting up a class

Import whatever your student information system exports — comma, tab, or semicolon; Perm ID or Local ID; one name column or last-plus-first; Period 3 or P3 or 3rd. Students appear on the roster with their period before any of them has signed in, so the first day of term isn't an empty page. A row with no ID number is listed and skipped rather than guessed at, and re-importing never deletes anyone or overwrites a period you set by hand.

Assigning work

Lists can be set for the whole class, for a period, or for one student, and the Assign board shows the entire class as one grid — students down the side, lists across the top. A dashed cell means the student is following their period; a gold cell is a choice made for them specifically, so who has been pulled out of their group is answerable at a glance. Nothing is written until you press Save, and the bar at the bottom counts what's pending.

A period can also run an ordered course instead of a flat set of lists. Where it does, the site moves students along it on its own — the next step opens when the current one is 80% solid — and a finished list stays in rotation rather than being taken away. A student who hasn't reached a step simply doesn't see it; there are no locked tiles, because a wall of things you can't do yet is not a thing to put in front of this class.

What it tells you

  • What should change — at most one line per student, and nothing for most of them. "Stuck on cards" for a student rating themselves Not yet thirty times, with a one-click switch to Say it on the same words. "These two in the wrong order" when Match It was assigned before the cards are solid. "Coasting." "No round in more than seven school days" — school days, so the whole class doesn't go quiet every weekend.
  • Ready to move up — "Marcos · Red Words 🃏 · finished List 2 (18 of 20 solid) → add List 3", with a button that stages it. The bar is four words in five, not all of them, so one stubborn word doesn't hold a student on a list for a term. It never advances anyone on its own.
  • Trouble spots — the same words across the class, ranked by how many students are struggling with each rather than by raw accuracy, so one student's bad day doesn't top the list. This is the "what do I reteach tomorrow" view.
  • By pattern, not just by word — thirty words all failing on the same vowel team is one problem with one lesson behind it. A second table groups by initial blend, final blend, vowel team oi/oy, multisyllable, irregular, nonsense CVC, with the most common error kind beside each.
  • Per student — hardest words worst-first, a per-list breakdown with the slow count, fluency trend lines, and the last few things the microphone heard for each missed word. A word that keeps coming back as "bread" is a reading error worth a lesson.
  • Notes — a private line per student for what the numbers can't say: "reads well, freezes when timed". This is the one thing on the site a student cannot read about themselves.
  • Two CSV exports — a roster row per student, and the long file: one row per student, list, and word attempted.

What it deliberately doesn't do

  • No passages. This is word-level practice. An earlier build had connected text, but the vocabulary rule that kept the passages honest also made them read like nothing anyone would write. Real prose deserves a better answer than that one.
  • No stored recordings. A recording of a fifteen-year-old struggling to read is not a thing to keep — the moment it's kept, somebody has to decide who may hear it.
  • No automatic promotion. Where a period isn't running a course, moving a student to the next list is always a teacher's click. "Solid on a screen" is not always solid on paper.
  • No percentage next to a name. Students see words solid out of words seen, not a score — a percentage in this context reads as a grade.
  • The card score is a self-rating. It measures honesty as much as reading. Nothing can watch a student read Wednesday and tell you whether they knew it on sight or worked it out — so the game asks them, and frames the payoff as the deck shrinking rather than the score going up. Match It is the checked version of the same skill when you want one.
  • Chrome only for anything that uses the microphone, and the whole site is behind a school Google sign-in — there's no guest mode, because per-student tracking is the point.

Everything above is live on the site today. The quickest way to see it is to have a student sign in and open one list two ways — Cards and Match It on the same words — and watch how differently the two go.
