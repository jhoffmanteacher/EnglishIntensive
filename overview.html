The basic shape

Each student has their own lists, and each list can be practiced more than one way.

A student signs in and sees only the lists their teacher has assigned them, so two students in the same room can be working on different things. Each mode is tracked as its own skill: reading moist cold, spelling it, and picking it out of a row of look-alikes are three separate things to be good at, and the site keeps them apart.

A round is 18 words chosen for that student, not the whole list (see How a round gets picked). One minute is the exception: it runs the whole list.

Five decodable families plus ten lists of irregular high-frequency words.

Why the nonsense list has no Match It

Match It says a word and asks the student to find it. Handed "vab", a speech synthesizer guesses — often "verb" — so the answer key would be wrong. A list with no meanings can be read, not heard.

The seven ways to practice

All but One minute score the same way — points, streaks, and 0–3 stars — so a student moving between modes meets one game asking different questions.

When a student gets it wrong

A red ✗ says they were wrong. It doesn't say which part — and for a reader guessing at blends or sliding off vowels, that is the only part worth knowing.

On the reveal, Say it lines up what the microphone heard against the word on screen, sound by sound, and names one thing. Never three, because a wrong reading usually has one cause. The letters that spell that sound light up gold.

Real phoneme audio, not "buh"

A speech synthesizer asked for /b/ says "buh", and "buh-a-tuh" does not blend into bat. So every sound the site plays is its own clip, one for each of the 37 sounds it can name, with the stop consonants trimmed back to the burst.

On the back of a card and on the Say it reveal, each syllable — or each sound of a one-syllable word — is pressable: it plays itself, then the whole word. A student who has been told a word twice and still can't read it usually can't hear which part is wrong. Pressing "tas" and hearing /t/ /a/ /s/ answers that without anyone having to explain it.

Hear yourself

After any word, a button plays back the last few seconds of the student's own reading, then reads the word properly. There is no arguing with the recording. The clip is never stored or uploaded. It exists in memory while that card is on screen and is gone when the next one arrives.

Red words and heart letters

A word is irregular, but it is rarely irregular all the way through.

said is s + d with one impossible middle. could is c + d with one. The back of the card marks that middle in red, under "In red: the part to remember by heart". The front stays plain, because the card has to be read cold first.

The point is the size of the thing being memorized. "Learn s-a-i-d" is four letters with no pattern. "said has an ai in the middle" is one chunk, and the s and the d are just reading. Which part can't be sounded out is marked by hand: that is a teaching judgement, not something software should guess.

How a round gets picked

Eighteen words drawn from the list, weighted by how that student is actually doing.

  • How badly it's going. A word missed every time is about six times as likely to come up as one that's solid. Accuracy is smoothed, so one lucky guess isn't mastery and one slip isn't a crisis.
  • Whether it's had its rest. Each word sits in a spaced-repetition box earning breaks of 0, 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 days. Inside its break a word is quieted rather than banned, so short sessions don't loop the same handful.
  • First tries only. Getting a word right after being told it isn't knowing it. A miss drops a word one box rather than back to zero, because a reset turns one fumble into a week of that word every session.
  • Speed, on the cards. A word decoded in three seconds is not a sight word, however reliably it comes back right. Flips are timed; over 2.5 seconds a word counts as slow and keeps coming round after its accuracy has stopped moving.
Slow is the scheduler's business, and yours

Students see a "⚡ fast" pill for a quick flip and no slow pill, because being told you were slow makes the next card slower. Slow shows up on your screen instead: the per-list breakdown reads "12 / 20 (4 slow)", and nobody is suggested for the next list on the strength of words they can decode but not read.

Say it, Cards and Match It also offer a 🔁 Comeback words warm-up of whatever this student has been missing lately. Cards adds a ⚡ Speed round once eight words are solid: nothing but words they already own, flipping themselves after two seconds. Useless as practice, and the only thing on the site that asks whether a word can be read without thinking about it.

The one-minute word-rate check

Every other mode asks whether a student knows a word. This one asks how fast — the question still worth asking after accuracy has stopped moving.

Words in rows of five, sixty seconds, the deck cycling so a fast reader never runs out. Scored in correct words per minute against a target of 60, with the change since last time. The clock starts on the first word actually read, not on the button, so fumbling with headphones doesn't count against anyone.

The listening is built for the way these students actually read. A hesitation, a repeated word, a false start, a skipped word, or the recognizer splitting "hubcap" into "hub cap" does not knock the rest of the run out of alignment; a genuine misread still counts as one. Every run is kept, up to a term's worth, and the dashboard draws each list as a small trend line, because one number says nothing about whether anything is changing.

It's a rate, not fluency in the full sense

A word list measures automaticity of word recognition — whether a word comes back without effort — which is what timed word-list tests like TOWRE assess. It is not prosody, phrasing, or holding meaning across a sentence. A passage hands a weak decoder context to lean on and a list takes it away, so a good rate here is not evidence that a paragraph goes well. Passage reading wants its own probe.

Built for these readers specifically

The audience is 9th and 10th graders practicing skills usually taught much younger.

  • Nothing is coded elementary. No grade labels, no cartoon imagery, no "Big Words" — the list is called Multisyllable. The progress graphics are a race car and a neon vault run.
  • 👁 Reading view, on every start screen. Four switches: font (site default, Lexend, or Atkinson Hyperlegible, drawn so b/d/p/q and I/l/1 can be told apart), wide letter spacing, all-lowercase, and cream cards instead of dark. The fonts ship with the site, so a network that blocks Google Fonts can't take them away from the one student who needs them.
  • 🔊 Read directions aloud on every game, in the same voice as the words. The directions are short and plain on purpose: this is a phonics class, and the instructions should not themselves be a decoding challenge.
  • Right and wrong are shown, not just heard — a green ✓ or red ✗ over the word — so nothing depends on hearing. The spoken coach is off by default.
  • Silence is never a wrong answer, and neither is a cough or an "um". The mic modes excuse a response that is all noise rather than marking a word nobody tried to read.
  • Mic-free options everywhere. Cards and Match It work in a loud room and on a Chromebook with a dead microphone.

What the teacher side does

One dashboard, four tabs, and a bias toward showing you the next action rather than every number.

Setting up a class

Import whatever your student information system exports — comma, tab, or semicolon; Perm ID or Local ID; one name column or last-plus-first; Period 3 or P3 or 3rd. Students appear on the roster with their period before any of them has signed in. A row with no ID number is listed and skipped rather than guessed at, and re-importing never removes anyone.

Assigning work

Lists can be set for the whole class, for a period, or for one student. The Assign board shows the class as one grid, students down the side and lists across the top. A dashed cell means the student is following their period; a gold cell is a choice made for them specifically, so who has been pulled out of their group is answerable at a glance. Nothing is written until you press Save, and the bar at the bottom counts what's pending.

A period can also run an ordered course instead of a flat set of lists. The next step opens when the current one is 80% solid, and a finished list stays in rotation rather than being taken away. A student who hasn't reached a step simply doesn't see it: no locked tiles, because a wall of things you can't do yet is not a thing to put in front of this class.

What it tells you

  • What should change — at most one line per student, and nothing for most of them. Stuck on a card list — thirty answers and still under 40% solid — with a one-click switch to Say it on the same words. Match It assigned before the cards are solid. Everything solid and nowhere to go. No round in more than seven school days, so the whole class doesn't go quiet every weekend.
  • Ready to move up — "Marcos · Red Words 🃏 · finished List 2 (18 of 20 solid) · add List 3", with a button that stages it. The bar is four words in five, so one stubborn word doesn't hold a student on a list for a term. The count in brackets is words solid and at pace, the same count the bar is measured against, with any slow-but-right words named beside it: "(16 of 20 solid, 3 more slow)". It never advances anyone on its own.
  • Trouble spots — the same words across the class, ranked by how many students are struggling with each rather than by raw accuracy, so one student's bad day doesn't top the list. This is the "what do I reteach tomorrow" view.
  • By pattern — thirty words all failing on the same vowel team is one problem with one lesson behind it. A second table groups by initial blend, final blend, vowel team oi/oy, multisyllable, irregular, nonsense CVC, with the most common error beside each.
  • Per student — hardest words worst-first, each list as solid / total with the slow count, reading-rate trend lines, and the last few things the microphone heard for each missed word. A word that keeps coming back as "bread" is a lesson.
  • Notes — a private line per student for what the numbers can't say: "reads well, freezes when timed". The one thing on the site a student cannot read about themselves.
  • Two CSV exports — a roster row per student, and the long file: one row per student, list, and word attempted.

What it deliberately doesn't do

  • No passages. This is word-level practice. An earlier build had connected text, but the vocabulary rule that kept the passages honest also made them read like nothing anyone would write.
  • No stored recordings. A recording of a fifteen-year-old struggling to read is not a thing to keep. The moment it's kept, somebody has to decide who may hear it.
  • No automatic promotion. Outside a course, moving a student to the next list is always a teacher's click. "Solid on a screen" is not always solid on paper.
  • No score next to a name. A student's list reads "12 of 20 solid", not a percentage, because a percentage there reads as a grade. The only percent a student sees is for the round they just played.
  • The card score is a self-rating. It measures honesty as much as reading. Nothing can watch a student read Wednesday and tell whether they knew it on sight or worked it out, so the game asks them, and frames the payoff as the deck shrinking rather than the score going up. Match It is the checked version of the same skill.
  • Chrome only for anything that uses the microphone, and the whole site is behind a school Google sign-in. There's no guest mode, because per-student tracking is the point.

The quickest way to see it is to have a student open one list two ways — Cards and Match It on the same words — and watch how differently the two go.
