Reading intervention · grades 9–10

Updated 13 October 2026

English Intensive

A practice website for high school students who are still learning to read words. Students practice sounding out words and reading common irregular words on sight. A microphone listens to them read, and a teacher dashboard shows which students and which words need reteaching.

How it works

Each student practices only what their teacher assigns.

  • A student signs in and sees only the activities their teacher assigned. Until the teacher assigns something, the home page is empty.
  • Students in the same room can work on different lists.
  • Each activity is scored separately. Reading moist aloud, spelling it, and finding it among similar words count as three different skills.
  • A round is 18 words picked for that student, not the whole list (see Which words come up). The one-minute reading check is the exception: it uses the whole list.

Made-up words: the computer never says them

No Match It. Match It says a word out loud and the student finds it. A computer voice can't reliably say a made-up word — given vab, it often says "verb" — so the answer would be wrong.

No spoken word in any activity. There is no Hear it button. After two misses the word is shown and the student can tap its sounds, but the whole word is never read aloud. The student reads; the computer only listens.

Graded by sound. Speech recognition always writes down the closest real word. So a made-up word counts as right if it is within one sound of the target. Real words must match exactly.

The seven activities

Every activity except One minute is scored the same way: points, streaks, and 0–3 stars.

When a student reads a word wrong

A red ✗ shows the word was wrong, but not which part. Say it also shows which part.

After a miss, Say it compares what the microphone heard with the word on screen, sound by sound. It points out one problem — the most likely cause — and highlights those letters in gold. The messages are:

Recorded speech sounds

A computer voice asked to say /b/ says "buh," and "buh-a-tuh" does not blend into bat. So the site plays a recording of each speech sound — 37 in all — with sounds like /b/ and /t/ cut short so there is no extra "uh."

On the back of a card, and after a missed word in Say it, students can tap each syllable (or each sound, in a one-syllable word) to hear it, then the whole word. This helps a student find which part they are getting wrong.

Red words don't have this. Their spelling doesn't match their sounds, so sounding them out would teach the wrong thing. Red word cards have no tap-to-hear, and Say it doesn't point out a wrong part on a red word. Instead, the card shows which letters to memorize (see Red words below).

Hear yourself

After any word, a button plays back the last few seconds of the student reading, then reads the word correctly. The recording is never saved or uploaded. It is deleted when the next word appears.

Red words: the letters to memorize

Most irregular words are irregular in only one spot.

said is a regular s and d with an unexpected ai in the middle. could is a regular c and d with an unexpected oul. On the back of the card those letters are red, with the note "Remember the red letters. They don't sound the way they look." The front of the card is plain, so the student tries to read it first.

This makes the job smaller. Memorizing s-a-i-d is four letters with no pattern. Remembering "said has ai in the middle" is one thing; the s and the d are regular. The red letters were chosen by hand for each word, not by software.

Which words come up

Each round is 18 words from the list, picked by how that student is doing.

  • Missed words come up more. A word missed every time is about six times as likely to come up as a word the student knows well. One lucky guess or one slip doesn't change much.
  • Known words wait longer. After each right answer, a word waits longer before it comes back: 1, 2, 4, 8, then 16 days. A miss shortens the wait by one step instead of starting over. During its wait a word can still come up, just less often, so short sessions don't repeat the same few words.
  • Only the first try counts. Getting a word right after being shown the answer doesn't count as knowing it.
  • Speed counts on Cards. Card flips are timed. A word that takes more than 2.5 seconds is marked slow, and slow words keep coming back even when the student gets them right.
Students are never told a word was slow

Students see ⚡ fast when they flip a card quickly, but never "slow" — being told you were slow tends to make the next card slower. Slow words show on the teacher dashboard instead, for example "12 / 20 (4 slow)". A student isn't suggested for the next list based on words they read right but slowly.

Say it, Cards and Match It also have a 🔁 Words you missed button, which practices only the words this student has missed recently. Cards adds a ⚡ Speed round once eight words are solid: only words the student already knows, and each card flips by itself after two seconds. It checks whether the student can read those words instantly.

The one-minute reading check

The other activities check whether a student knows a word. This one checks how quickly they read words.

  • Words are shown in rows of five. The student reads aloud for 60 seconds; the list starts over if they reach the end.
  • The score is correct words per minute. The target is 60, or 40 on the made-up words, where every word has to be sounded out. The student also sees the change since last time.
  • The timer starts when the student reads the first word, not when they press Start.
  • Normal reading doesn't throw off the scoring: a pause, a repeated word, a false start, a skipped word, or the microphone hearing "hubcap" as "hub cap." A misread word counts as one miss.
  • Every run is saved, up to a term's worth. A run where the internet dropped or the microphone heard nothing is shown to the student but not saved.
  • The dashboard shows each list's runs as a small trend line.
This measures word reading speed, not reading fluency

A word list shows how quickly a student recognizes single words — what timed word-list tests like the TOWRE measure. It does not measure expression, phrasing, or understanding. A passage gives a struggling reader context to help them; a list doesn't. So a good score here doesn't mean passage reading is going well. Check passage reading separately.

Made for older students

The students are 9th and 10th graders practicing skills usually taught in elementary school.

  • Nothing looks like elementary school. No grade labels, no cartoons, no "Big Words" — the list is called Multisyllable. The progress graphics are a race track and a maze.
  • 👁 Reading view on every start screen. Four settings: font (site default, Lexend, or Atkinson Hyperlegible, where b/d/p/q and I/l/1 look clearly different), wider letter spacing, all lowercase, and cream cards instead of dark ones. The fonts are stored on the site, so a school network that blocks Google Fonts can't remove them.
  • 🔊 Read directions aloud on every activity, in the same voice as the words. Directions are short and plain so they aren't a reading challenge themselves.
  • Right and wrong are shown, not only heard — a green ✓ or red ✗ over the word — so nothing depends on hearing. The spoken coach is off by default.
  • Silence is never wrong, and neither is a cough or an "um." The microphone activities ignore noise instead of marking the word wrong.
  • Activities without a microphone. Cards and Match It work in a loud room or on a Chromebook with a broken microphone.

The teacher dashboard

Four tabs — Students, Assign, Periods, Trouble Spots — built to show what to do next rather than every number.

Setting up a class

Import the roster file your student information system exports. It can be comma, tab, or semicolon separated; use Perm ID or Local ID; have one name column or separate last and first; and write periods as Period 3, P3, or 3rd. Students appear with their period before they ever sign in. A row with no ID number is listed and skipped. Importing again never removes anyone.

Assigning work

You choose each student's activities. There is no class-wide default: a student with nothing assigned sees an empty home page, and the dashboard tells you who. Periods are only for grouping students on the dashboard.

The Assign tab shows the class as a grid — students down the side, lists across the top. Click a cell to choose which activities one student gets for one list. To change many students at once, check their names (or a whole period) and choose Add to what they have, Take these away, or Replace all their games. You can assign work to students from the roster before they sign in. Nothing is saved until you press Save; the bar at the bottom shows how many changes are waiting.

A student can't open an activity they weren't assigned, even from a copied link. They're sent to their own activities instead.

What it shows

  • What should change — at most one suggestion per student, and none for most students. For example: stuck on a card list (thirty answers and still under 40% solid), with a one-click switch to Say it on the same words; Match It assigned before the cards are solid; every word solid with nothing next; or no practice in more than seven school days, so weekends don't count.
  • Ready to move up — a student who has 4 of every 5 words on a list solid, e.g. "Marcos · Red Words 🃏 · finished List 2 (18 of 20 solid) · add List 3". Add it puts the next list on the Assign tab; it isn't saved until you press Save. The count only includes words that are solid and not slow; slow words are listed beside it: "(16 of 20 solid, 3 more slow)". The site never moves a student up by itself.
  • Trouble Spots — the words the class is missing, ranked by how many students are struggling with each, so one student's bad day doesn't top the list. Use it to decide what to reteach tomorrow.
  • By pattern — the same results grouped by what each list teaches: starting blends, ending blends, oi/oy, multisyllable, irregular words, and made-up words. Each row shows the most common error, except irregular words, where no error is named. Thirty words all missed on the same vowel is one problem with one lesson.
  • Per student — hardest words first, each list as solid / total with the slow count, reading-speed trend lines, and the last few things the microphone heard for each missed word. A word that keeps being heard as "bread" tells you what to teach.
  • Notes — a private note per student for what the numbers don't show, e.g. "reads well, freezes when timed." Students never see it.
  • Two spreadsheet downloads (CSV) — one row per student, and a detailed file with one row per student, list, and word attempted.
Terms on the dashboard

Solid — the student reads the word right at least 9 times in 10, with enough recent first-try right answers that it has reached the longest wait between practices.

Shaky — right less than 60% of the time.

Slow but right — right, but card flips average more than 2.5 seconds.

Answers and accuracy count first tries only.

What it doesn't do, on purpose

  • No reading passages. This is practice with single words. An earlier version had passages, but limiting them to words students had practiced made them read unnaturally.
  • No saved recordings. A recording of a teenager struggling to read should not be kept. Once it's kept, someone has to decide who may hear it.
  • No automatic move-up. Moving a student to the next list is always the teacher's choice. Solid on screen isn't always solid on paper.
  • No percentage next to a student's name. A student's list shows "12 of 20 solid," not a percentage, because a percentage looks like a grade. The only percentage a student sees is for the round they just finished.
  • Card scores are self-reported. On Cards, the student marks whether they read the word right. Software can't tell whether a student knew Wednesday on sight or sounded it out, so it asks. The reward is a shrinking pile of cards, not a higher score. Match It checks the same skill without relying on the student's word.
  • Chrome only for anything that uses the microphone. The whole site requires a school Google sign-in; there is no guest mode, because the point is tracking each student.

The quickest way to see the site: have a student practice one list two ways — Cards and Match It on the same words — and compare how differently they go.
